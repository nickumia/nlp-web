Note
This blog presents an example through personal experience. I hope you can introspect as necessary to apply the lessons to your own life.

Swimming has dominated this era of my life as the preferred form of exercise. It's been a few years since I successfully swam for the first time and there's been this recurring problem that I only recently found a solution to and came to terms accepting it as the solution. Since the early days of swimming, my stamina has improved quite a bit. As such, I can do more laps now. The problem is simple.

I simply kept losing count of how many laps I had completed.

One of the reasons I love swimming is because my mind has the space and freedom to wander. Compared to running, where I definitely focus on my breath or zone out gazing around. Or compared to biking, where my vision blends reality together at high speeds. When I swim, there's no exciting scenery nor any stretching of reality. My mind mostly becomes fluid like the water and swims freely through ideas.

Due to this fact, needing to keep a rigid series of numbers in my head became more and more difficult as I acclimated to the nature of swimming. I wouldn't forget the count every lap. Just often enough that somewhere in the middle of a workout I'd find myself wondering, Was that 18? Or 19?

Ironically, the first solution I fantasized about was an ultrasonic sensor (or similar) that detected by presence at the edge of the pool and automatically counted each lap. It would show the count on this fancy display and it would be super cool!

But, as most inventions go, the reality/practicality of the implementation was messier. It needed waterproofing. It needed calibration. It needed an energy source. It would need maintenance. ... and, if you can tell from my mentality when I go swimming, none of that aligns with the simplicity of doing laps.

The solution should match the activity.

It was only very recently that I had an extra small PVC-like tube and saw the seams in the pavers around the pool and had a tiny eureka moment. I counted the number of seams. 28 on each side. Enough to do 50 laps (+6 extra). So I tried it out. After each lap, I'd slide it over one tile. Then one more. And one more. Until I reached the end.

Simple.

It worked.

The engineering ideas were genuinely exciting. I still think they would be fun projects. But I never built them. And I don't need to. As cool as they are, they overcomplicate a simple problem. Other than being a lazy developer (as all developers are), there was a simple question that gave the obvious answer:

What problem am I trying to solve?

The original problem was that I couldn't reliably keep track of my laps. The little marker solved that in the least invasive way possible. So what was the Arduino solving? What value was the sensor adding? I wasn't so sure anymore. That question has stayed with me because I think it's one we don't ask often enough.

When we talk about technology, we usually start with what's possible.

Could this be automated?

Could I build an app for this?

Could AI help?

As an engineer (and designer), I love those questions. But, since the road trip, I've started wondering how to ask the other question that belongs before them:

What does this give me? What does it ask of me?

Sometimes the answer is obvious.

A washing machine gives me hours of my time back.

GPS lets me explore places I'd never confidently navigate on my own.

A calendar remembers things so I don't have to.

In those cases, technology quietly fades into the background and lets me focus on living.

Other times, the answer isn't as clear.

Sometimes the new technology saves a few seconds while asking for batteries, updates, charging, troubleshooting, setup, or just another thing to think about.

Sometimes it solves a problem I don't really have anymore.

And sometimes, if I'm honest with myself, I'm not trying to solve a problem at all.

I'm just fascinated by the idea.

There's nothing wrong with that.

Building things simply because they're interesting has taught me an incredible amount throughout my life. But I've realized that's a different reason than building something because I genuinely need it.

The distinction matters.

Lately, whenever I catch myself imagining a new gadget, a new automation, or a clever piece of software, I've started pausing for just a moment.

Not to ask whether I can build it.

To ask what role I want technology to play.

Do I want it to replace something tedious? To make room for something more meaningful? To help me do something I couldn't do before? Or am I simply enjoying the process of creating?

None of those answers are wrong. I just think they're worth knowing. Technology is one of the most powerful tools we've ever created.

The challenge isn't deciding whether to use it.

The challenge is learning when it truly serves us.
